    Four Roads, One Family

    Our ancestors came from the Scottish Highlands, the Presbyterian north of Ireland, County Limerick and Germany. Their paths ran through the Blue Ridge, the Tug Valley of the Hatfields and McCoys, and the farm country of Ohio and Missouri before they met in southwest Iowa. This is what the records say, and where the records still go quiet.

    Quick answers

    • Are we Scottish? Yes, on both sides. Mom's Scott line traces to Scotland by family tradition. Dad's Fleming, McCunn and Carmichael lines are Scots-Irish: Scottish families who lived in Ulster before coming to America.
    • Are we Irish? Yes. Grandpa Fred's mother was a Handley, and her grandfather John Handley was born in County Limerick in 1836.
    • Are we German? Yes. The Conrad name comes from Jacob Conrad, who arrived from Germany in 1868 at age 14.
    • Are we Hatfields or McCoys? Both, through Mom's side. The next page explains exactly how.
    • Where did the two sides meet? Both of Dad's grandparents' families lived around Clarinda, Iowa, and Grandma Delphia worked there too. Arizona is where the family lives today.
    The question everyone asks

    How We Are Both Hatfields and McCoys

    Pike County, Kentucky, and the Tug Valley · 1786 to 1927

    This is the story of America's most famous family feud, told from inside it. For more than a hundred years, our family stood on both sides of the Tug Fork. The McCoy blood came first, long before the shooting started. The Hatfield blood came last, long after it ended. Both meet in one person: Grandpa Buff Scott Jr.

    Part one · 1780s to 1820s

    Over the mountains

    After the American Revolution, families from Virginia's Piedmont pushed west over the Appalachian Mountains in search of land they could afford. Many were Scots-Irish: Protestant Scots whose families had been settled in Ulster, in the north of Ireland, before crossing to America. They brought clan loyalty, a distrust of distant government, and a habit of settling scores themselves.

    Three of those pioneer families are ours. "Old William" McCoy, a Revolutionary War soldier, settled in the hills of eastern Kentucky. Ephraim Hatfield, born in 1765 in Bedford County, Virginia, the same county our Scotts came from, ended his life on Blackberry Creek. And the Scotts followed the same trail into the valley of the Tug Fork, the river that today separates Kentucky from West Virginia.

    Where our family fits: around 1810, Billy Scott married Elizabeth, who family genealogies say was Old William McCoy's daughter. From then on, our Scotts were McCoy kin, fifty-five years before the feud began.
    Part two · 1820s to 1860

    One valley, two states

    The Tug Valley was steep, isolated and poor in cash but rich in timber. Families farmed narrow creek bottoms, raised hogs that roamed free in the woods, and married their neighbors. The McCoys settled mostly on the Kentucky side in Pike County, which was formed in 1821. The Hatfields settled mostly on the Virginia side in Logan County. That side became West Virginia in 1863.

    A river between them meant two sets of courts, two sheriffs and two governors. That would matter later: a man could commit a crime on one bank and be out of reach on the other.

    Where our family fits: Ephraim Hatfield had two families. His first wife's line led to Devil Anse Hatfield, who would lead the Hatfields in the feud. His son Jeremiah, born in 1807, is our ancestor. Jeremiah married Rachel Vance, and that marriage gave us a second road to Devil Anse: Rachel's grandfather, Abner Vance, was also Devil Anse's great-grandfather. Our family is tied to the Hatfield leader twice, once through the Hatfields and once through the Vances.
    Part three · 1861 to 1865

    The Civil War splits the valley

    When the Civil War came, the mountains did not divide neatly. Most Hatfields and McCoys sided with the Confederacy, and Devil Anse led a local Confederate guerrilla band called the Logan Wildcats. Randolph McCoy's brother, Asa Harmon McCoy, joined the Union army instead.

    In January 1865, Harmon came home wounded and was hunted down and shot in a cave near his home. Suspicion fell on the Wildcats, and especially on Devil Anse's uncle, Jim Vance. No one was ever tried. Many historians mark this as the first blood of the feud, though at the time it looked like one more wartime killing.

    Part four · 1878 to 1880

    A pig, a trial and a love story

    In 1878, Randolph McCoy accused Floyd Hatfield of stealing one of his hogs. The case went before a local justice of the peace, a Hatfield, with a jury split between the families. A McCoy relative, Bill Staton, testified for the Hatfields. Juror Selkirk McCoy voted with them too, and Floyd won. In 1880, Staton was shot dead by two of Randolph's nephews, who were acquitted on a claim of self-defense.

    That same year, Devil Anse's son Johnse Hatfield met Randolph's daughter Roseanna McCoy at an election-day gathering, and she ran off to live with the Hatfields. Johnse never married her. In 1881 he married her cousin Nancy McCoy instead. Roseanna's story became the "Romeo and Juliet" legend people still tell.

    Where our family fits: the 1880 census lists Henderson Scott, then 27, his father Daniel Scott (Randolph McCoy's first cousin), and Randolph's family all in the same Blackberry district of Pike County. Our Scotts were neighbors and kin of the McCoys while all this was happening.
    Part five · August 1882

    Election day and the pawpaw trees

    Election days were the biggest gatherings in the mountains: voting, trading, whiskey and fights. On election day in August 1882, at Blackberry Creek in Pike County, three of Randolph's sons, Tolbert, Pharmer and young Randolph "Bud" McCoy, got into a fight with Devil Anse's brother Ellison Hatfield. They stabbed him many times and shot him.

    Kentucky officers arrested the three brothers, but Devil Anse's men took them away from the officers and held them on the West Virginia side. When Ellison died, the Hatfields brought the boys back across the river into Kentucky, tied them to pawpaw trees, and shot them. From this point on, it was a true blood feud.

    Part six · January 1888

    The New Year's night attack

    For years, no one was punished. Then Perry Cline, a Pikeville lawyer related to the McCoys by marriage, pushed Kentucky to revive the old murder charges and put rewards on the Hatfields. Cline had lost a large tract of land to Devil Anse in an earlier lawsuit, so the feud was also about land and money.

    To stop the McCoys from testifying, a Hatfield party led by Jim Vance, who was Devil Anse's uncle and our Rachel Vance's first cousin, and Cap Hatfield attacked Randolph McCoy's cabin on New Year's night in 1888 and set it on fire. Randolph escaped, but his daughter Alifair and son Calvin were killed, and his wife, Sarah, was beaten and left badly injured.

    The country was horrified. Kentucky posses began crossing into West Virginia to capture Hatfields without legal papers. They killed Jim Vance and fought the Hatfields at the Battle of Grapevine Creek that same month. The two states took the dispute all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court. In May 1888 it ruled that the men brought back to Kentucky could be tried there, even though the way they were captured was irregular.

    Where our family fits (family lore, not yet proven): stories handed down say Henderson Scott was once shot after being mistaken for a McCoy, and his daughter Parlee left the McCoy home the day before it burned. These stories are family tradition, not proven by records. No record shows any of our direct ancestors fighting in the feud.
    Part seven · 1889 to 1891

    Trials, a hanging and a slow peace

    The trials were held in Pikeville. Eight Hatfield supporters were convicted. Seven got life in prison, among them Devil Anse's brother Valentine "Wall" Hatfield, who died in prison. Ellison Mounts, believed to be Ellison Hatfield's son, was hanged in Pikeville on February 18, 1890. By 1891, the killing had largely stopped.

    Devil Anse was never brought to trial. He was baptized in 1911 and died in 1921. Randolph McCoy, who lost five of his children, died in 1914. By then, the railroad and the coal companies had arrived in the Tug Valley. Many historians argue that land, timber and coal mattered as much as old grudges.

    Part eight · 1891 to today

    Enemies become family

    With the violence over, the valley went back to doing what it had always done: marrying its neighbors. In 1923, Buff Scott Sr., who descended from the McCoy side through his great-great-grandmother Elizabeth, married Maude Hatfield, a distant cousin of Devil Anse. In 1927, their son, Grandpa Buff Scott Jr., was born. He was the first person in our line with both McCoy and Hatfield blood.

    The families have long since made peace. In 1979 they faced each other on the TV game show Family Feud, and on June 14, 2003, descendants of both families signed a formal truce in a public ceremony.

    Where our family fits: Mom, you and your children all descend from both feuding families. Our family is the feud's ending in one person, Grandpa Buff, two warring families made one.

    What we know and what is only a story

    Proven by records: the 1880 census shows the Scotts and Randolph McCoy in the same district. Buff Sr. married Maude Hatfield. Grandpa Buff descends from both.

    Probable, connecting the dots from family trees: Elizabeth McCoy was Randolph McCoy's aunt, which makes Randolph our first cousin, six times removed. And Maude Hatfield's great-great-grandfather Ephraim Hatfield was also Devil Anse Hatfield's great-grandfather, which makes Devil Anse our distant cousin (a second cousin, four times removed, or a half second cousin if Jeremiah and Valentine had different mothers). A second, separate link runs through the Vances: Rachel Vance's grandfather, Abner Vance, was Devil Anse's great-grandfather, which makes Devil Anse also our second cousin, five times removed, and the raid leader Jim Vance our first cousin, six times removed. These links come from family genealogies. The 1850 census strengthens the Hatfield link: Ephraim's 95-year-old widow, Anna, was living in Jeremiah's home three years after Ephraim died. No original record yet names Jeremiah's or John Wesley Vance's father outright.

    Family lore, not yet proven: Henderson was shot after being mistaken for a McCoy. His daughter Parlee left the McCoy home the day before it burned. His son Julius married Preacher Anse Hatfield's daughter. No record shows any of our direct ancestors fighting in the feud.

    Why feuding families ended up related

    The Tug Valley was a small world. The same families had lived along the Tug Fork, the river between Kentucky and West Virginia, for generations. People married their neighbors, so Hatfields, McCoys, Scotts, Staffords, Vances and Blackburns were already tangled together long before 1865. Some McCoys even sided with the Hatfields during the feud. Once the violence ended, marrying across the old line was normal again.

    The feud leader

    William Anderson "Devil Anse" Hatfield (1839–1921)

    Devil Anse led a Confederate guerrilla band in the Civil War and then built a profitable timber business on thousands of acres of virgin forest. He won much of that land from Perry Cline, a McCoy relative, in a lawsuit. He led the Hatfield side of the feud, was never tried, and was baptized late in life. Several of his sons became lawmen: Joe and Tennis Hatfield both served as sheriff of Logan County, and Elliott became a physician.

    How he is related to you: second cousin, four times removed, through Ephraim Hatfield (a "half" second cousin if Jeremiah and Valentine had different mothers, which means you would share only Ephraim instead of a couple). "Four times removed" means you are four generations further down from Ephraim than he is.
    The governor and senator

    Dr. Henry Drury Hatfield (1875–1962)

    Devil Anse's nephew took the opposite road from his uncle. He studied medicine at the University of Louisville and New York University and became a coal-company doctor and chief surgeon for the Norfolk and Western Railway, the line that opened the Tug Valley's coal fields.

    In 1913 he became Governor of West Virginia, in the middle of the violent Paint Creek and Cabin Creek coal strike. He settled it by pardoning the labor organizer Mother Jones and other leaders, and he signed the state's first workers' compensation law. He also declared martial law and arrested strikers, so both sides judged him harshly. In World War I he served as a major leading an Army base hospital in France. From 1929 to 1935 he was a United States Senator, then settled in Huntington, where he ran a hospital and owned farmland.

    How he is related to you: third cousin, three times removed (a "half" third cousin if Jeremiah and Valentine had different mothers).
    The wealthiest cousin

    Hazel Hatfield Fairless

    Henry D.'s daughter Hazel married Benjamin F. Fairless in 1944. Fairless, the son of an Ohio coal miner, had trained as a civil engineer and rose to become president of U.S. Steel from 1938 to 1955, and its chairman and CEO from 1952 to 1955. U.S. Steel was then the biggest steel company in America. Its steel went into the ships, tanks and weapons of World War II. Its Fairless Works mill in Pennsylvania, and the town of Fairless Hills that grew up beside it, were named for him.

    That makes Hazel the wealthiest relative we have found in any line of the family. The money came through her marriage. She is the blood relative; Fairless married in.

    How she is related to you: fourth cousin, twice removed (or half fourth cousin, by the same rule).
    The second road

    Abner Vance and "Uncle Jim" (1760–1819 and the 1880s)

    Our ancestor Jeremiah Hatfield married Rachel Vance. Her grandfather was Abner Vance, remembered as a Baptist preacher in the Virginia mountains. When a man named Daniel Horton ran off with Abner's daughter Betty and then abandoned her, Abner shot Horton's brother Lewis. He fled to the Tug Valley, came back to face trial, and was hanged at Abingdon, Virginia, in 1819. Legend says he wrote a lament in his jail cell, one of the earliest songs composed west of the Alleghenies. Historians still argue over the details of the story.

    Betty Vance's daughter Nancy married "Big Eaf" Hatfield and became Devil Anse's mother. Betty's son was Jim Vance, the "Uncle Jim" who led the 1888 New Year's raid on the McCoy cabin and was killed by a Kentucky posse a few weeks later. Rachel's father, John Wesley Vance, was Betty's brother.

    How they are related to you: Abner Vance is your 6th great-grandfather. Jim Vance is your first cousin, six times removed. Through this road, Devil Anse is also your second cousin, five times removed. You are tied to the Hatfield leader twice, through his father's family and through his mother's.
    Read this before you brag

    The caveat

    Henry D. and Hazel connect to you through one link: that Jeremiah Hatfield, Maude's great-grandfather, was Ephraim Hatfield's son. Family trees say he was, the dates and places fit, and the 1850 census shows Ephraim's 95-year-old widow living in Jeremiah's home, which is strong evidence. But no record names Jeremiah's father outright. The Vance road depends on compiled family notes that make Rachel's father a son of Abner Vance. Treat everyone on this page as probable. Devil Anse is the safest bet, because two separate roads lead to him. If either road is proven wrong, he still stays on this page through the other one.
